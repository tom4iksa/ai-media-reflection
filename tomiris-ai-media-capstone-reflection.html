A Capstone Shows You What a Catalog Can’t

I sat in on the AI and Media minor capstone to see where that path really leads.

Tomiris Tutkabayeva
6 min read · Nov 2026

The Projection Theater is built for watching stories on a big screen. On Monday, November 23, it held a different kind of screening. For one hour over lunch, graduating seniors in the AI and Media minor presented their capstone projects to the room.

I was not there as a senior. I am a sophomore in Journalism and Strategic Communication, and I have not yet declared a minor. My choice is between two paths: AI and Media, and Strategic Communication. I came with a practical question. I wanted to see where the AI and Media path actually leads, two years from now, when it is my turn to stand at the front.

I took a seat a few rows back, in one of the theater’s black leather armchairs. Stage lights hung from the ceiling, and the giant screen glowed in purple and blue. At the podium, a presenter in a black suit walked the room through her slides. A second speaker sat on stage in an armchair beside a small vase of white flowers. Around me, a few people followed along with laptops open on their knees.

What a capstone reveals about a minor

A course catalog tells you what a program promises. A capstone shows you what it delivers. The AI and Media minor is built from six electives that students choose themselves, and the final presentation is the moment those choices have to add up to something.

Because every student assembles a different set of courses, no two routes into the capstone look the same. I came to see that the minor is less a fixed track than a lens. It can be pointed at journalism, design, data or ethics, depending on who is holding it.

That flexibility cuts both ways. It lets a student shape the minor around a genuine interest. It also asks a great deal of the student, because no one hands over a finished structure. The capstone is where you must explain, in public, why your choices belonged together.

The second thing I noticed was the balance between building and questioning. The minor is designed to pair technical skill with ethical judgment. One project showed that meeting point more clearly than any other.

The project that stayed with me

Two small labels sat above the slide’s title: “Telehealth” and “Comm Exchange.” The second suggested to me that the project grew out of the presenter’s Communication Exchange quarters on Northwestern’s Evanston campus. The title was Hims & Hers, the American telehealth company. Beneath it was a single design question: “How might we centralize data for Jane?”

Jane, as far as I could tell, was not a patient. The slide mapped “a day at work” for her, which made her a persona for someone inside the company. I read her as an employee whose information was scattered across too many places. The method was stated plainly: four expert interviews and eleven user-testing interviews, followed by a prototype. The tools were AI insights, a dashboard prototype and user testing. A laptop mock-up of the dashboard’s sign-in screen sat beside them.

The conclusion fit on one line. Centralized data, access codes and AI summaries keep teams aligned.

What struck me first was where the AI sat. I had expected this minor’s capstones to focus on AI-generated content, such as synthetic images, automated articles or chatbots. Here, AI was not the product facing an audience. It was a quiet layer inside a workflow, condensing information so that a team could act on it. AI as internal communication is a far less visible use of the technology, and probably a far more common one.

The most consequential AI in media may be the kind no audience ever sees.

The second thing that struck me was the order of the work. Before building anything, the presenter talked to people, experts first and users second. The AI came last, shaped by what those interviews revealed. That is the order I try to follow as a reporter. It is also the order many of the startups I cover reverse.

I also left with questions. Health data is among the most sensitive information a company holds. Centralizing it makes a team faster, but it also creates a single point of exposure. I suspect the access codes on the slide were an answer to exactly that worry. The AI summaries raised a harder question for me. A summary of a patient’s case is only useful if it is accurate, and a confident error in medicine costs far more than one in a news brief. I also wondered how far eleven interviews can carry a design meant for a company of that size.

These questions did not feel like criticism. If anything, they were the questions the minor itself says it wants its graduates to ask.

What it means for my journalism, and my choice

I write analytical features about AI startups and technology for Forbes Kazakhstan. In that work, I interview founders who describe their products with complete confidence. My job is to test that confidence. To do it well, I need to understand what a model does, what data it relies on and where it fails, without depending on the founder’s own explanation.

Hims & Hers is precisely the kind of company I might profile. From the outside, I would read its earnings, its funding history and its marketing. The capstone showed me the inside view: one employee, a fragmented working day, and a tool designed around her.

Sitting in the theater, I realized how often I borrow that understanding instead of owning it. I can take apart a startup’s business model in detail. I am far less able to judge whether its technology does what the pitch deck claims. The gap between reporting on AI and understanding AI is the one I most need to close.

I had expected this event to show me where the two minors separate. Instead, the Hims & Hers project sat right on the border between them. Its problem was internal communication, and its answer used AI. That overlap changed my question. The choice is less about which field I want to work in, and more about which skill I am missing.

Strategic Communication would sharpen skills I already use every week: reading an audience, shaping a message, planning a campaign. Those skills matter, and my current strategic communication coursework has shown me how much craft lies behind them. Yet that minor would deepen a strength rather than repair a weakness.

AI and Media points directly at the weakness. I have already taken the first steps on that road. My Introduction to Python and AI course counts toward the minor, and my AURORA research at AIM-Lab has me studying how language models could support journalists. The capstone showed me where those steps could lead: to a body of work I could explain and defend in front of a room.

···

I came to the capstone to see where the AI and Media path leads. I left with an answer that is less about careers and more about credibility.

In the next decade, the journalists who cover technology well will be the ones who can look inside it.

That belief now shapes my decision, and I am leaning firmly toward the AI and Media minor. Before spring registration, I will meet my advisor to map out the six electives, with Data Journalism and Automated Journalism at the top of my list. I will also keep one habit from this event. Whenever I write about an AI company, I will ask not only what it promises, but what it would have to prove in front of an audience like that one.

Two years from now, I hope to be the one presenting in the Projection Theater. I also hope a sophomore in the back row is asking me the same hard questions I asked this week.
